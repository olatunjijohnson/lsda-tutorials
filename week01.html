Longitudinal Data Analysis: week 1 tutorial

Week 1 — Exploring longitudinal data

NoteHow to use this page

Each exercise below is a live R session. Type your answer and press Run; you can experiment freely, nothing is saved and nothing can break. The first block takes a few seconds to start while R loads in your browser.

Worked solutions are on Canvas.

Q1: This question relates to the cattle data, available in the course package as cattle.

  1. Consult the R online manuals, specifically “An Introduction to R” (PDF file), Section 7, titled “Reading Data from Files.” Proceed to import the dataset into R.
The datasets come with the course package rather than as files, so you want
`data()`, not `read.table()`. Afterwards, `dim()` and `head()` tell you the
shape and the first few rows.
What this shows

60 rows and 12 columns: one row per animal, the first column the treatment group and the remaining eleven the weights. Every animal has all eleven, so the design is balanced — which is what lets us treat the data as a 60 by 11 matrix at all.

  1. Generate a spaghetti plot for the cattle data.
`cattle` is in wide format: one row per animal, one column per time. So a
"spaghetti plot" means drawing one line per row.
Start the plot with the first animal using `type = "l"`, then add the rest in a
loop with `lines()`. Set `ylim` from the whole dataset so no trajectory is cut
off, and remember column 1 is the group indicator, not a weight.
What this shows

Each line is one animal. Two things are worth noticing. Weight increases steadily for everyone, so there is a clear time trend. And the lines stay roughly parallel: animals that start heavy stay heavy. That persistent ordering is the within-subject correlation, and it is why treating these 660 measurements as independent would be wrong.

  1. Calculate the sample means for observations at each time point. Additionally, compute the sample covariance matrix and sample correlation matrix.
All three summaries operate on the weight columns only, so drop the group
column first with `cattle[, -1]`.
`sapply()` applies `mean` down each column; `cov()` and `cor()` take the whole
matrix at once.
What this shows

The means rise steadily with time, as the plot suggested.

In the correlation matrix, read along any row. Correlation is high next to the diagonal — adjacent times are near 0.9 — and falls away as the gap widens, down to around 0.5 between the first and last times. That decay with lag is the pattern AR(1) is built for, and it is why compound symmetry, which forces every pair to share one correlation, would fit these data badly.

  1. Create a pairwise scatter plot for the cattle data. Analyse and describe the key features of the observed correlations.
`pairs()` draws the full scatterplot matrix in one call. Again, exclude the
group column.
Look along the diagonals: how does the spread change as the gap between two time
points grows?
What this shows

Four times are shown rather than all eleven, because an 11 by 11 grid of panels is unreadable. The panels for adjacent times are tight, near-linear clouds; those for widely separated times are much more diffuse. This is the same information as the correlation matrix, seen directly.

  1. Divide the cattle dataset into two groups, Group A and Group B. For each group, determine the sample means at each time point, as well as the sample covariance and sample correlation.
`which(cattle[, 1] == 0)` gives the row numbers for one group. Use that to
subset, then repeat everything from item 4 on each group separately.
Two plots side by side is `par(mfcol = c(1, 2))`.
What this shows

Group A starts heavier. By the last time point the two groups have converged and crossed over, so the difference between them is not constant — it changes with time. A model with only a group effect could not represent that; you need an interaction between group and time, which is what you fit in week 3.

  1. Produce pairwise scatter plots for each group. Identify and discuss any similarities or differences between the two groups evident in the plots.
You already have the two group subsets from item 6, so this is `pairs()` twice.
Give each a `main` title so you can tell them apart.
What this shows

The correlation structure looks similar in both groups: the same tightening near the diagonal and loosening away from it. That matters for modelling, because it means one covariance structure can reasonably be assumed to hold for both groups rather than fitting each separately.

  1. Plot the sample means of both groups against time within a single figure. Provide your interpretations and conclusions drawn from the figure.
Plot the first group's means with `plot()`, then add the second with `lines()`
in a different colour. Both means are vectors of length 11 that you computed in
item 6.
What this shows

The two mean curves cross. Group A is heavier at first, its growth flattens after about time 4, and group B overtakes it. This crossing is the treatment effect, and it is a difference in slope rather than in level.

Q2 You will work with longitudinal data on lung function growth in school-age girls from Topeka, Kansas, as part of the Six Cities Study of Air Pollution and Health. The dataset contains repeated pulmonary function measurements from 299 girls, typically enrolled around ages 6-10 and followed annually until high school graduation or loss to follow-up. The number of observations per individual ranges from 1 to 12, making the dataset unbalanced with respect to age.

At each visit, lung function was measured using simple spirometry, and the outcome of interest is the logarithm of the ratio of forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) to height, i.e., log(FEV1/height).

The dataset is available in the course package.

Key variables include:

- id: unique identifier for each girl
- ht: height (m)
- age: age at measurement (years)
- baseht: height at the first visit
- baseage: age at the first visit
- fev1: volume of air exhaled in the first second (litres)
- logfev1: logarithm of fev1
- logfev1_ht: pre-computed log(fev1/height)
  1. Load the dataset and check the structure of the data. How many observations are there in total? How many individuals?
`fev1` is in long format: one row per visit, not per girl. So `nrow()` is the
number of observations, and the number of girls is the number of distinct `id`
values.
What this shows

1993 observations from 299 girls — about 6.7 visits each on average, but that average conceals a lot, as the next item shows.

  1. Summarise the number of observations per individual. What does this tell you about the balance of the data?
Count the rows per `id`, then summarise those counts. A histogram of them shows
the shape of the follow-up.
If every girl had the same count, the data would be balanced — what do you see?
What this shows

The distribution is right-skewed. Most girls have several visits, but some have only one or two. So the data are unbalanced: \(n_i\) differs between girls.

This is not a defect and does not need fixing. It does have two consequences. The design matrices differ in size between girls, so the model has to be written per subject. And a girl with two visits carries far less information than one with twelve, which is exactly what produces shrinkage in the week 3 predictions.

  1. Produce a plot of log(fev1 / height) against age for 50 randomly selected girls. Connect points for the same individual. Comment on the variability between individuals and the measurement patterns over age.
Draw a random sample of `id` values first, then keep only the rows belonging to
them. In `ggplot`, `group = id` is what stops the lines being joined into one
long zigzag across girls.
Set a seed so your plot is reproducible.
What this shows

The spaghetti plot for the unbalanced case. Compared with the cattle data, notice that the lines start and stop at different ages and have different numbers of points — that is the imbalance made visible.

There is substantial variation between girls in both level and slope, which is the motivation for allowing a random intercept and a random slope in week 2.

  1. Compute and plot the average log(fev1 / height) at each age, aggregating across individuals.
Group by `age` and take the mean of `logfev1_ht` within each. Plotting the
result gives the population-average growth curve.
Ages are not on a common grid, so some ages are supported by very few girls —
worth bearing in mind when you read the plot.
What this shows

Averaging within each age gives the population-level growth curve, which rises steadily through adolescence.

Ages were rounded before averaging because girls were not measured on a common grid; without rounding, most ages would be supported by a single girl. Even so the extremes are based on few observations, so treat the ends of the curve with caution.

Week 1 complete

What you have learnt

You have worked with two longitudinal datasets and seen the feature that motivates the whole unit: observations on the same subject are not independent.

  • A spaghetti plot shows every subject’s trajectory at once. Lines that stay ordered — heavy animals staying heavy — are correlation you can see by eye.
  • The sample correlation matrix over time puts a number on it. Correlation was strong between adjacent times and decayed as the gap widened. That decay is what AR(1) will model in week 2.
  • Balanced and unbalanced designs. The cattle data are balanced: every animal measured at the same 11 times. The FEV1 data are not — girls contribute between 1 and 12 visits at ages that do not line up. That difference changes what the design matrices look like and which methods are convenient.
  • Group comparisons. Splitting by treatment and comparing means over time is the crudest form of the analysis the rest of the unit does properly.

The thing to carry forward: if you analysed either dataset with ordinary linear regression, you would be assuming away the correlation you have just spent an hour looking at. The standard errors would be wrong.

Check your understanding

Three questions on what you have just done. Not recorded, and you can retry as often as you like.

1. Looking at the correlation matrix you computed for the cattle weights, the correlation between times 1 and 2 was much higher than between times 1 and 11. What does that pattern tell you?

This decay with lag is the signature the AR(1) structure is built to capture, and it is why compound symmetry — which forces every pair to share the same correlation — would fit these data poorly. Nothing here says anything about reliability, group means or balance.

2. Your histogram of observations per girl in the `fev1` data was right-skewed, with most girls having several visits and a few having only one or two. What is the practical consequence for modelling?

Unbalanced is normal, not a defect — mixed models handle it directly. What it does mean is that $n_i$ differs between girls, so $\mathbf{X}_i$ and $\mathbf{Z}_i$ differ in dimension, and a girl with two visits carries far less information than one with twelve. That difference is exactly what produces shrinkage in week 3. Discarding data to force balance would throw away information for no gain.

3. You plotted the group means against time and they diverged after time 4. What would you need in the model to represent that?

Diverging means are a difference in *slope*, which is what a time-by-group interaction represents. A random intercept lets animals differ in level, not in trend, and an AR(1) structure describes the correlation of the errors rather than the mean. This interaction is the term you fit in week 3, and it is the parameter the experiment exists to estimate.
