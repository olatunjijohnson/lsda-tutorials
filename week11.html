Spatial data analysis: week 11 tutorial

Week 11 — Spatial point processes

NoteHow to use this page

Each exercise below is a live R session. Type your answer and press Run; you can experiment freely, nothing is saved and nothing can break. The first block takes a few seconds to start while R loads in your browser.

Worked solutions are on Canvas.

In this tutorial sheet, you will learn the following:

  • Load and visualize a spatial point pattern dataset,
  • Explore first- and second-order properties,
  • Fit a homogeneous and inhomogeneous Poisson model,
  • Assess model goodness-of-fit.

In this tutorial, we analyse the spatial distribution of nests of two ant species, Messor wasmanni and Cataglyphis bicolor, recorded at a site in northern Greece. The dataset ants from the spatstat.data package contains locations of 97 ant nests within a sampling window.. You can load the data using

  1. Visualize the ant nest spatial point pattern data from the spatstat.data package?
`data(ants)` from `spatstat.data`. `plot()` shows the pattern and `summary()`
gives the window, the number of points and the average intensity.
Look at the pattern first and form an opinion before you compute anything: does
it look clustered, regular, or neither?
What this shows

The ant nest pattern, and the summary gives the window, the number of points and the average intensity.

Look at the plot and form an opinion before computing anything: does it look clustered, evenly spread, or neither? Point pattern analysis rewards having a prior expectation, because several of the statistics are easy to misread.

  1. Perform exploratory data analysis on the point pattern, including quadrat counting and kernel intensity estimation?
Two complementary views. One function divides the window into a grid and counts points per cell — crude
but easy to test. Another gives a smooth kernel estimate of the intensity
surface; it is the `ppp` method of a familiar function.
The bandwidth matters: too small and you see noise, too large and you smooth the
structure away.
What this shows

Two complementary views of the first-order structure — how the density of points varies across the window.

Quadrat counts are crude but directly testable. Kernel intensity is smooth and easier to look at, but the bandwidth is a choice: too small and you are looking at noise, too large and you smooth the structure away.

Neither says anything about whether points interact with one another. That is second-order structure, and it comes later.

  1. Fit spatial point process models to the data, starting with a homogeneous Poisson process and then extending to an inhomogeneous Poisson process with spatial covariates?
`ppm(ants, ~1)` fits a homogeneous Poisson process — constant intensity, points
independent. `ppm(ants, ~ x + y)` lets the log intensity vary linearly across
the window.
The second is still a Poisson process: points remain independent given the
intensity. Only the first-order structure has changed.
What this shows

The homogeneous model assumes constant intensity; the inhomogeneous one lets the log intensity vary linearly across the window.

Both are still Poisson processes, and this is the point students most often miss: in both, points are placed independently given the intensity. Only the first-order structure has changed. Neither model contains any interaction between points.

  1. Assess the goodness-of-fit of the fitted models using quadrat tests?
There is a test that compares observed and expected counts per quadrat. A small
p-value says the homogeneous Poisson model does not fit — but it will not tell
you whether the cause is a trend in intensity or genuine interaction between
points.
What this shows

A small p-value says the homogeneous Poisson model does not fit.

It does not say why. The test compares observed and expected counts per cell, so it responds to a varying intensity and to interaction between points in exactly the same way. To distinguish them you need the K-function — and even that needs care, as item 7 shows.

Note also the warning about small expected counts: the chi-squared approximation is unreliable when quadrats contain few points, which is a reason to be cautious about the exact p-value rather than just the conclusion.

  1. Analyze the second-order properties of the point pattern using the K-function, and interpret whether the pattern is clustered, regular, or random?
`Kest()` estimates Ripley's K, and `envelope()` gives simulation bounds under
complete spatial randomness.
Above the theoretical line means clustering, below means regularity. Ant nests
compete for space, so think about which you would expect before you look.
What this shows

Ripley’s K with a simulation envelope under complete spatial randomness.

Above the theoretical curve suggests clustering, below suggests regularity or inhibition. For ant nests, competition for space and forage makes regularity the biologically plausible expectation.

Challenge

  1. Fit a model with a quadratic trend surface using x + y + I(x^2) + I(y^2) as covariates. Compare it with the linear model.
`ppm(ants, ~ x + y + I(x^2) + I(y^2))`. The `I()` is needed so `^` means power
rather than formula-crossing.
Compare with the linear-trend fit using `AIC()`. A quadratic surface can capture
a ridge or a hollow that a plane cannot.
What this shows

The quadratic trend surface can capture a ridge or a hollow in intensity that a plane cannot. Compare with the linear fit by AIC.

Be careful about what this shows: a better-fitting intensity surface tells you the points are unevenly distributed, not that they interact.

  1. Simulate 99 point patterns from your best model and compare their envelope with the observed K-function using envelope().
The same envelope function you used in item 5 accepts a *fitted model* in place
of the point pattern, in which case it simulates from that model rather than
from complete spatial randomness. That is the sharper test: it asks whether
anything is left unexplained once the trend is accounted for.
If the observed K now sits inside the envelope, the inhomogeneity explained the
apparent clustering.
What this shows

This is the sharpest idea in the week.

The envelope is now simulated from the fitted inhomogeneous model rather than from complete spatial randomness. That changes the question from “are these points arranged as if uniform and independent?” to “is there anything left once the trend is accounted for?”.

It matters because a varying intensity alone produces more close pairs than CSR predicts, and so makes an independent pattern look clustered. Comparing against CSR confounds first-order and second-order structure. If the observed K now sits inside the envelope, the apparent clustering was inhomogeneity all along.

This confounding is the most common real error in point pattern analysis, and it is worth remembering well beyond this course.

Week 11 complete

What you have learnt

You have analysed the third type of spatial data, where the locations themselves are the random quantity.

  • A different question. For geostatistical and areal data the locations are given and the values are random. For a point pattern the locations are the data, and the question is whether they are clustered, regular or random.
  • First-order versus second-order. Intensity is how many points per unit area and how that varies across the window; interaction is whether points attract or repel each other. They are genuinely different, and are easily confused.
  • Complete spatial randomness is the homogeneous Poisson process, and it is the null against which everything else is judged.
  • Quadrat tests are simple but blunt: a small p-value tells you the homogeneous Poisson model fails without saying whether the cause is a trend in intensity or interaction between points.
  • Ripley’s K with a simulation envelope separates the two. Above the theoretical curve suggests clustering, below suggests regularity.
  • The important subtlety. Fitting an inhomogeneous Poisson process and then re-testing with an envelope simulated from that model asks whether anything is left once the trend is accounted for. Apparent clustering often turns out to be a varying intensity rather than interaction — the classic confounding of first and second order.

Check your understanding

Three questions on what you have just done. Not recorded, and you can retry as often as you like.

1. Your quadrat test on the ant nests returned a small p-value. What can you conclude?

The quadrat test compares observed and expected counts per cell, so it detects departures from constant intensity and independence *together*. A small p-value says something is wrong without saying what. Separating first-order from second-order structure needs the K-function, and ideally an envelope simulated from a fitted inhomogeneous model.

2. Your K-function fell below the theoretical CSR curve over a range of distances. What does that suggest?

$K(r)$ is proportional to the expected number of further points within distance $r$ of a typical point, so falling below the curve means points are avoiding one another. For ant nests that is biologically plausible: colonies compete for space and forage territory.

3. After fitting an inhomogeneous model, you re-ran `envelope()` using that fitted model rather than CSR. Why does that matter?

More points in one part of the window means more close pairs than CSR predicts, and so an apparently clustered pattern, even when points are placed independently given the intensity. Simulating from the fitted model asks the sharper question: is anything left once the trend is accounted for? This confounding of first and second order is the most common real error in point pattern analysis.
