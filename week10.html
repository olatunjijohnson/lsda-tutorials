Spatial data analysis: week 10 tutorial

Week 10 — CAR and ICAR models

NoteHow to use this page

Each exercise below is a live R session. Type your answer and press Run; you can experiment freely, nothing is saved and nothing can break. The first block takes a few seconds to start while R loads in your browser.

Worked solutions are on Canvas.

In this tutorial sheet, you will learn the following:

  • how to fit a spatial model to area-level data;
  • how to perform spatial predictions and visualise summaries of the predictive distribution

We analyse the Lip cancer data in scotland. The data contain, for each of the Scotland counties, the number of observed and expected lip cancer cases among males in Scotland between 1975-1980, and a variable that indicates the proportion of the population engaged in agriculture, fishing, or forestry (aff). The aff variable is related to exposure to sunlight which is a risk factor for lip cancer. The data also contain the shapefile of the Scotland counties and everything can be found in scotland_lip

  1. Fit a model assuming that the observed cancer cases, ( Y_i ), are conditionally independently Poisson distributed:

[ Y_i (E_i _i), i = 1, , n, ]

where ( E_i ) is the expected cancer cases and ( _i ) is the relative risk in county ( i ). The logarithm of ( _i ) is expressed as:

[ (_i) = _0 + _1 _i + u_i + v_i, ]

where:

  • ( _0 ) is the intercept representing the overall risk,
  • ( _1 ) is the coefficient of the covariate ( _i ),
  • ( u_i ) is a spatially structured random effect modeled using a Conditional Autoregressive (CAR) distribution: [ u_i u_{-i} N({u}_{_i}, ), ]
  • ( v_i N(0, ^2_v) ) is an unstructured spatial random effect.
Two steps. First build the adjacency matrix from the polygons, as in week 9 —
and note the geometry is in metres, so a `snap` tolerance must be in metres too.
Then `S.CARleroux()` with `family = "poisson"`, the AFF covariate, and
`offset(log(expected))`.
The offset is what turns a count model into a relative risk model: without it
you would be modelling raw counts rather than counts relative to what is
expected.
  1. Map the relative risk of lip cancer and their uncertainty for each of the counties. Interpret the result noting that the relative risk quantifies whether county \(i\) has higher (\(\theta_i>1\)) or lower (\(\theta_i<1\)) risk than the average risk in the standard population.

Challenge

Relative risk is the fitted value divided by the expected count.
`model$samples$fitted` holds the posterior samples, so the posterior mean gives
the estimate and the posterior standard deviation gives the uncertainty.
Map both, and compare the smoothed map with the raw SIR from week 9. The
smoothing is the model borrowing strength from neighbours — strongest where the
expected counts are small and the raw SIR is therefore noisiest.
  1. Map the probability that the relative risk is greater than 2.
You have posterior samples of the relative risk, so the probability that it
exceeds 2 is just the proportion of samples above 2, computed area by area.
This is the kind of statement a public health decision actually needs: not a
point estimate, but how confident we are that risk is elevated.
  1. Fit another model where ( u_i ) is modeled using a Intrinsic Conditional Autoregressive (CAR) distribution
Same call, but the ICAR model is the limiting case with `rho` fixed at 1 — see
`S.CARleroux`'s arguments. Compare the fitted risks: ICAR assumes the spatial
structure is entirely local, so it tends to smooth more.

Week 10 complete

What you have learnt

You have fitted Bayesian spatial models to area-level counts.

  • The hierarchy. Counts are Poisson given a relative risk; the log relative risk is a covariate effect plus a spatially structured random effect plus an unstructured one.
  • The offset does the work. offset(log(expected)) is what turns a model for counts into a model for relative risk. Without it you are modelling how many cases occur, not whether that is more than expected.
  • CAR models borrow strength. The conditional autoregressive prior says an area’s risk is like its neighbours’. The result is a smoothed map — and the smoothing is strongest exactly where the raw SIR was least reliable, in areas with small expected counts.
  • Structured and unstructured effects. The first is spatially correlated, the second is area-specific noise. The Leroux parameterisation lets the data decide the balance; ICAR fixes it at purely spatial.
  • Posterior samples answer real questions. From the samples you can compute the probability that relative risk exceeds 2 area by area. That is a far more useful statement for a public health decision than a point estimate.
  • MCMC needs checking. Burn-in, thinning and convergence are not formalities.

Check your understanding

Three questions on what you have just done. Not recorded, and you can retry as often as you like.

1. Comparing your map of smoothed relative risk with the raw SIR map from week 9, which counties changed most?

Where the data are informative, the likelihood dominates and the estimate stays near the raw SIR. Where the expected count is small the likelihood is weak, the CAR prior pulls harder, and the estimate is drawn towards the neighbourhood average. This is the same borrowing of strength as BLUP shrinkage in week 3 — and it is the reason for fitting the model rather than mapping raw ratios.

2. What would happen if you omitted `offset(log(expected))` from the model?

The offset is what converts a model for counts into a model for relative risk. Without it, counties with more people have more cases and the map becomes a population map. The offset carries a coefficient fixed at 1 rather than estimated, which is precisely what makes $\theta_i$ interpretable as a ratio to expectation.

3. You computed the posterior probability that relative risk exceeds 2 for each county. Why is that more useful to a public health team than the posterior mean?

A posterior mean of 1.9 could come with high or low confidence and the number alone does not say which. $P(\theta_i > 2 \mid \text{data})$ answers "how sure are we that risk here is at least doubled?", which is the question that actually determines whether to act. Being able to compute any functional of the posterior from the samples is one of the practical advantages of the Bayesian approach.
