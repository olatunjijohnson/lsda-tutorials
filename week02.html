Week 2 tutorial: Linear mixed models

Week 2 — Linear mixed models

NoteHow to use this page

Worked solutions are on Canvas.

Overview

Week 1 showed that measurements on the same subject are correlated. The linear mixed model is the first tool for doing something about it: a random effect \(\mathbf{b}_i\) lets each subject have its own intercept and slope, and \(\mathbf{R}_i\) describes what correlation is left over within a subject once that is accounted for.

By the end of this sheet you should be able to:

  • write a linear mixed model in matrix form and say what each matrix holds;
  • fit one with lme(), choosing a structure for \(\mathbf{R}_i\);
  • compare covariance structures with likelihood ratio tests and information criteria; and
  • interpret the fitted fixed effects and variance components.

The data

fev1 records lung function growth in 299 school-age girls from Topeka, Kansas, followed as part of the Six Cities Study of Air Pollution and Health. Each girl was enrolled at around age 6 to 10 and measured roughly annually, but at ages of her own: the number of observations per girl ranges from 1 to 12, so the data are unbalanced and there is no grid of common time points. There are 1993 measurements in all.

Column Description
id identifier for each girl
age age at measurement, years
ht height at measurement, metres
baseage, baseht age and height at the first visit
fev1 forced expiratory volume in one second, litres
logfev1 log of fev1
logfev1_ht log(fev1/ht), the response modelled here

Q1: fitting and comparing linear mixed models

For the fev1 data, consider a linear mixed model of the form \[ \log\left( \frac{\text{FEV}_1}{\text{Height}} \right)_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Age}_{ij} + b_{0i} + b_{1i} \text{Age}_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ij}, \] where

  • \(\log\left( \frac{\text{FEV}_1}{\text{Height}} \right)_{ij}\): the response for individual \(i\) at time \(j\);
  • \(\beta_0\): fixed intercept;
  • \(\beta_1\): fixed slope for age;
  • \(b_{0i}\): random intercept for individual \(i\), capturing between-subject variation;
  • \(b_{1i}\): random slope for age for individual \(i\);
  • \(\varepsilon_{ij}\): within-subject residual error.

Note that \(\mathbf{b}_i \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{G})\) and \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_i \sim N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{R}_i)\).

  2. In R, use the function lme() to analyse the data by specifying \(\mathbf{G}\) as general covariance structure and \(\mathbf{R}_i\) as

    2.4. ARMA(1,1) structure,

    where the option method in R is the maximum likelihood (ML) estimation method. Select the best structure of \(\mathbf{R}_i\) in terms of the resulting AIC or BIC value.

What this shows

Four models differing only in correlation. update() avoids retyping the call, so the one thing that changes stays visible.

The two sets of fixed effects printed at the end are almost identical. Changing the assumed error correlation barely moves the estimates; it moves the standard errors. That is the same lesson GEE makes explicit in week 5.

One thing worth knowing, because it will bite you if you try it. The question specifies a random intercept and a random slope, but fitting random = ~ age | id together with corAR1 or corARMA fails to converge on these data. That is not a bug in your code: a random slope already makes the marginal correlation change with the gap between visits, and AR(1) errors do the same thing, so the two compete to explain the same structure and the likelihood has no well-identified maximum. Comparing R_i structures with the simpler G, then choosing G afterwards, avoids the problem — which is exactly why the tutorial splits the selection into two stages.

Spot the problem. One of the four models below should not have been fitted in this form. When you compare them, look closely at compound symmetry against independence, and explain what you see. Think about what covariance structure a random intercept produces on its own.

`AIC()` and `BIC()` both accept several fitted models at once and return a table.
Lower is better for both. If they disagree, remember BIC penalises extra
parameters harder.
What this shows

Both criteria are shown because they can disagree. Here they do not: AR(1) wins decisively on each, by around 400 units of AIC over independence. A gap that large is not a close call.

That result makes sense. The girls are measured roughly annually over several years, and residuals a year apart should be more alike than residuals five years apart, which is what AR(1) allows.

The problem to spot. Compound symmetry has exactly the same log-likelihood as independence, and an AIC exactly 2 higher: the penalty for one extra parameter that bought nothing. Its fitted correlation is 0. This is not because compound symmetry is the wrong shape for these data. A random intercept already gives compound symmetry, since \(\text{Cov}(\mathbf{Y}_i) = \sigma_b^2 \mathbf{J} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}\) (lecture, week 2). Adding corCompSymm on top asks for the same structure twice, so the random intercept absorbs all of it and the extra correlation has nothing left to explain. Fitting gls(..., correlation = corCompSymm(form = ~ 1 | id)) with no random effects gives the same log-likelihood again. The fair comparison for compound symmetry is therefore fit_ind itself.

What this shows

correlation is now fixed at AR(1) and random varies. The three G structures are nested: identity within diagonal within general.

Note control = lmeControl(opt = "optim"). With the default optimiser the general structure does not converge; switching optimisers fixes it. If a mixed model fails to converge, changing the optimiser is worth trying before concluding the model is wrong — though if several optimisers fail, that is a genuine signal of over-parameterisation.

In the printed variance components, pdDiag gives a standard deviation for the intercept and one for age but no correlation between them, because a diagonal G assumes that correlation is zero rather than estimating it.

`summary()` on the winning fit. Read three things: the fixed effects (what is

What this shows

The summary is the payoff. Three things to read.

Fixed effects. The coefficient on age is the population-average annual change in log(FEV1/height). It is positive, which is lung function growing through adolescence, as it should.

Random effects. The between-girl standard deviations, compared with the residual standard deviation below them. If the intercept SD is comparable to or larger than the residual SD, girls differ from one another about as much as a single girl varies between visits — which is what makes the mixed model worthwhile.

Correlation structure. The estimated AR(1) parameter Phi. A value well above zero says residuals remain related from one visit to the next even after the random effects have absorbed the between-girl differences.

When you write this up, state each of these in a sentence about lung function rather than about the model.

Q2: correlation structures when the times are not a grid

Everything above was fitted to 60 cattle weighed on the same eleven days, where corAR1() indexes observations 1 to 11 and \(\rho^{|j-k|}\) means what you expect. This section repeats the exercise on data with no common time points, where that reading of AR(1) is not available.

The cd4 data hold CD4+ counts for 369 men around HIV seroconversion, between 1 and 12 observations each, at 1342 distinct times. Weeks 2 and 3 model \(\sqrt{\text{CD4}}\), which stabilises the variance of a count-like response.

  1. Fit a random intercept model for \(\sqrt{\text{CD4}}\) on time, then add compound symmetry to it, and compare the two. What has the second model bought?
A random intercept is `random = ~ 1 | subject`.
Compound symmetry as an error correlation is `corCompSymm()`.
Before you run it, predict what will happen: what marginal correlation does a
random intercept already induce between two observations on the same man?
What this shows

The log-likelihood is -7275.77 for both, to two decimal places. The second model has one more parameter and has bought precisely nothing, so its AIC is exactly two worse: 14559.54 against 14561.54.

This is not a numerical accident and it is worth being sure you can explain it. A random intercept already makes every pair of observations on the same man equally correlated, with \[\mbox{Corr}(Y_{ij}, Y_{ik}) = \frac{\sigma_b^2}{\sigma_b^2+\sigma^2},\] which is compound symmetry. Adding corCompSymm on top asks the model to explain the same exchangeable correlation a second time. The two parameters are not separately identifiable, and the fit lands wherever it started.

The practical rule: a random intercept and compound symmetry are two names for one structure. Use one or the other, never both.

  1. Now replace compound symmetry with a correlation that decays with the time gap. Does that buy anything, and why could you not have used corAR1() here?
`corCAR1()` is the continuous-time version of AR(1): it uses
$\mbox{Corr} = \phi^{|t_{ij}-t_{ik}|}$ with the *actual* times, so the gaps need
not be equal and need not be integers.
`corAR1()` would use the position of the observation in the sequence instead —
first, second, third — which treats a two-week gap and a two-year gap as the
same thing.
The last line prints the estimated $\phi$.
What this shows
model df AIC logLik
random intercept 4 14559.54 -7275.77
+ compound symmetry 5 14561.54 -7275.77
+ continuous AR(1) 5 14393.34 -7191.67

The same one extra parameter that bought nothing as compound symmetry buys 84 log-likelihood units as a continuous AR(1), and AIC falls by 166. The difference between the two is only what the parameter is attached to: a constant correlation between all pairs, or a correlation that decays with the gap.

So the data say there is serial correlation here, over and above the men simply differing from each other. \(\hat\phi = 0.159\), and the correlation between two residuals a time \(u\) apart is \(0.159^{u}\) with \(u\) in years: about 0.16 at one year, 0.03 at two. It is short-range, which is why the random intercept alone could not represent it — that term is the same at every lag.

Why not corAR1(). It would raise the correlation to the power of the difference in position, so the gap between a man’s first and second visit would count as “one” whether it was three months or three years. With 1342 distinct times that is meaningless. corCAR1() reads the times from form = ~ time | subject and uses them.

The general lesson, which the cattle data hid: with equally spaced observations corAR1() and corCAR1() agree, and you can be careless. With unequal spacing only one of them is defensible.

Week 2 complete

What you have learnt

You have fitted linear mixed models and chosen between covariance structures.

  • Two sources of within-subject correlation. Shared random effects (\(\mathbf{Z}_i \mathbf{G} \mathbf{Z}_i^{\mathrm{T}}\)) and serial correlation in the errors (\(\mathbf{R}_i\)). The marginal covariance is their sum.
  • Structures for \(\mathbf{R}_i\). Independence, compound symmetry (all pairs equally correlated), AR(1) (geometric decay with the gap) and ARMA(1,1). The choice is a claim about how correlation behaves over time, not a technicality.
  • Structures for \(\mathbf{G}\). Identity, diagonal and general — increasingly permissive about how subjects vary in intercept and slope, and whether those two are related.
  • Choosing between them with AIC and BIC, remembering that BIC penalises extra parameters harder, so the two can disagree.
  • Interpretation. A fitted mixed model tells you both the population average trajectory and how much individuals depart from it.

Worth trying: run_app("cov_explorer") to see what each structure actually looks like as a matrix and as simulated trajectories.

Check your understanding

Three questions on what you have just done. Not recorded, and you can retry as often as you like.

1. Your AIC and BIC table for the four \(\mathbf{R}_i\) structures gave AIC lowest for ARMA(1,1) but BIC lowest for AR(1). Which is the more defensible conclusion?

Disagreement between AIC and BIC is common and is not a symptom of anything being wrong — it means the extra parameter bought a moderate improvement in fit. What matters in a report is that you state the criterion and the reasoning rather than quietly picking whichever answer you preferred. Nesting is not a problem for either criterion.

2. You fitted `fit_ind` with no `correlation` argument at all. What does that model assume about the residuals within a girl?

Omitting `correlation` sets \(\mathbf{R}_i = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}\). That does *not* mean the observations are independent: the random intercept and slope still contribute \(\mathbf{Z}_i\mathbf{G}\mathbf{Z}_i^{\mathrm{T}}\) to the marginal covariance. Confusing independent errors with independent observations is one of the most common misreadings of a mixed model.

3. In `summary(fit_G_diag)` the random effects table reported a standard deviation for the intercept and one for `age`, but no correlation between them. Why?

A diagonal G *assumes* zero covariance rather than estimating it and finding zero. If you believe girls who start with higher lung function also grow faster, that is a claim about the covariance, and you need the general structure to let the data speak to it.
