Spatial data analysis: week 9 tutorial

Week 9 — Areal data and Moran’s I

NoteHow to use this page

Worked solutions are on Canvas.

In this tutorial sheet, you will learn the following:

  • how to construct weight matrix; and
  • how to evaluate spatial correlation;

We analyse the Lip cancer data in scotland. The data contain, for each of the Scotland counties, the number of observed and expected lip cancer cases among males in Scotland between 1975-1980, and a variable that indicates the proportion of the population engaged in agriculture, fishing, or forestry (aff). The aff variable is related to exposure to sunlight which is a risk factor for lip cancer. The data also contain the shapefile of the Scotland counties and everything can be found in scotland_lip

  1. Load the scotland_lip dataset from the course package and check the columns of the data and ensure that you understand what each column means.
`data(scotland_lip)` — it is already an `sf` object, so the geometry comes with
it. Look at the columns before going on.
What this shows

An sf object: the attribute table and the county polygons together. cases and expected are the observed and expected counts, and aff is the proportion of the workforce in agriculture, fishing and forestry — a proxy for outdoor work and so for sunlight exposure.

  1. Calculate the standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) in the Scotland counties, defined as observed cases divided by expected cases, map and interpret it.
The SIR is observed over expected. There is a helper: `sir(cases, expected)`.
A value above 1 means more cases than the age-sex structure of that county would
predict. Map it with a diverging scale centred on 1, so above and below are
visually distinct.
What this shows

The SIR is observed over expected, so 1 means “as many cases as the county’s population structure predicts”.

Read the map with the next item in mind. The most extreme values tend to sit in counties with small expected counts, because an SIR based on 2 expected cases is extremely noisy — observing 4 gives an SIR of 2 with nothing unusual happening. That instability is the problem the CAR models in week 10 exist to solve.

  1. Plot the scatter plot of the SIR and aff. Is there any substantial association between the variables?
Scatter plot of SIR against `aff`. AFF is a proxy for outdoor work and hence
sunlight exposure, which is a known risk factor for lip cancer — so a positive
slope is the expected direction.
What this shows

A weak positive association, in the direction the science predicts: more outdoor work, more sunlight, more lip cancer.

The scatter is wide, so this is suggestive rather than conclusive on its own — and note that neither the SIR nor this relationship accounts for the spatial correlation you are about to detect.

  1. Calculate the neighbors of each of the regions of Scotland based on Queen contiguity and map the neighbors obtained.
`spdep` builds a neighbour list straight from polygons. Its `queen` argument
decides the rule: `TRUE` counts a shared corner as adjacency, `FALSE` requires a
shared edge.
Scotland has islands. If a region ends up with no neighbours, that is a real
feature of the geography, not a bug — though `snap` can bridge small gaps.
What this shows

Queen contiguity treats counties sharing a corner as neighbours. The plotted network shows how the counties connect.

Three counties end up with no neighbours at all: Orkney, Shetland and the Western Isles. That is not a bug — an island genuinely shares no boundary with anything. You have to decide what to do about it, and the choice affects everything downstream. Options are to allow empty sets with zero.policy = TRUE, to use a rule that always yields neighbours such as k nearest or a distance threshold, or to bridge the gap with a snapping tolerance, remembering that the tolerance is in the units of the geometry — metres here.

  1. Which county or counties has the largest number of neigbours.
`card()` gives the number of neighbours per region. Find the maximum and which
county attains it.
What this shows

The most connected county sits in the middle of the mainland, as you would expect. Under a binary weights matrix it will exert more total influence on the statistics than a peripheral county, which is one reason row standardisation is often preferred.

  1. Produce a map showing the wigtown county and its neighbors.
Find Wigtown's index, pull its neighbours out of the neighbour list, then map
the three categories: the county itself, its neighbours, and everywhere else.
What this shows

Wigtown and its neighbours, drawn to make the adjacency concrete. It is worth tracing one county by eye like this before trusting a 56 by 56 matrix built by a function.

  1. Construct the spatial weights matrix using the binary coding, and the row standardized (1/number of neighbors) coding.
`nb2mat()` with `style = "B"` for binary and `style = "W"` for row-standardised.
Row-standardised means each row sums to one, so a region with many neighbours
gives each of them less weight.
Islands with no neighbours need `zero.policy = TRUE`.
What this shows

Two encodings of the same neighbour list.

Binary gives every neighbour a weight of 1, so a county with eight neighbours contributes eight units of influence and one with two contributes two.

Row-standardised divides by the number of neighbours, so every county’s neighbours carry the same total weight of 1. Check the row sums to see this.

The choice changes Moran’s I and it changes what a CAR model smooths towards. It belongs in the methods section, not left implicit.

  1. Check for the evidence of spatial correlation using Moran I test?
Convert the neighbour list into a weights list object first, then pass it to
the Moran's I test function.
Positive Moran's I means nearby counties have similar SIRs. Check the p-value,
but also look at the statistic against its expectation, which is -1/(n-1) rather
than zero.
What this shows

Moran’s I with a small p-value: there is strong evidence of positive spatial autocorrelation, meaning neighbouring counties have similar SIRs.

Note the expectation reported alongside the statistic. Under the null, Moran’s I is not centred on zero but on \(-1/(n-1)\), about \(-0.018\) for 56 counties. Compare the statistic with that, not with 0.

This result is the justification for week 10. If neighbouring counties are genuinely alike, a model can borrow strength between them — which is what makes the unstable small-area SIRs estimable.

Challenge

  1. Calculate the neighbors of each of the regions of Scotland based on 3 nearest neighbors and map the neighbors obtained.
Two steps: find the k nearest centroids, then convert that to a neighbour list
with `knn2nb()`. Note this gives every region exactly three neighbours,
including the islands — which is either a feature or a distortion,
depending on what you think adjacency should mean.
What this shows

k nearest neighbours guarantees every county exactly three neighbours, islands included. That solves the empty-set problem, but at a price: adjacency is no longer symmetric in the way contiguity is, and a county can be “near” something across a substantial stretch of sea.

  1. Calculate the neighbors of each of the regions of Scotland based on distance and map the neighbors obtained.
The distance-based constructor in `spdep` takes a lower and an upper bound. The geometry is in
metres, so the bounds must be too.
What this shows

A distance threshold instead. The bound is in metres, because the geometry is. Choosing it is a modelling decision: too small and islands are isolated again, too large and everything is everyone’s neighbour.

  1. Calculate the neighbors of each of the regions of Scotland based on contiguity Neighbors of order 2 and map the neighbors obtained.
There is a `spdep` function for higher-order (lagged) neighbours. Order 2 means "neighbours of my
neighbours", which widens the definition of local considerably.
What this shows

Second-order contiguity means neighbours of neighbours. This widens the definition of “local” considerably and will smooth more aggressively in a model.

  1. Construct the spatial weights matrix using inverse distance values.
Compute inverse distances between centroids, then pass them to `nb2mat()` via
`glist`. Closer neighbours then carry more weight than distant ones.
What this shows

Inverse-distance weights let closer neighbours count for more, rather than treating all neighbours alike. It is the most intuitively appealing scheme of the four, though it introduces a dependence on the distance units and on how centroids were computed.

Taken together, items 9 to 12 make the real point of the week: there is no single correct W. Each encodes a different idea of what makes two areas neighbours, each gives different answers, and the choice must be reported and justified rather than accepted as a default.

Week 9 complete

What you have learnt

You have moved from point-referenced to area-level data, where “nearby” has to be defined rather than measured.

  • Standardised incidence ratios. Observed over expected, so a value above 1 is more cases than the population structure predicts. SIRs based on small expected counts are extremely noisy — which is exactly the problem CAR models solve next week.
  • Adjacency is a modelling choice. Queen or rook contiguity, k nearest neighbours, a distance threshold, or higher-order lags. Each encodes a different idea of what makes two areas neighbours, and each gives different answers.
  • Islands are real. Orkney, Shetland and the Western Isles have no contiguous neighbours at all. You either bridge them with a snapping tolerance, use a distance or k-nearest rule, or handle empty neighbour sets explicitly.
  • Weights matrices. Binary treats all neighbours alike; row-standardised gives each area’s neighbours equal total influence, so an area with many neighbours weights each one less.
  • Moran’s I. A global test for spatial autocorrelation. Note its expectation under the null is \(-1/(n-1)\), not zero.

The point to hold on to: every one of these results depends on the weights matrix you chose. Report that choice as part of the analysis.

Check your understanding

Three questions on what you have just done. Not recorded, and you can retry as often as you like.

1. When you built queen-contiguity neighbours, three counties came out with none. What is the correct response?

An island genuinely shares no boundary with anything, so the empty set is correct. Your options are to allow it with `zero.policy = TRUE`, to use a rule that always produces neighbours such as k nearest or a distance threshold, or to bridge the gap with a snapping tolerance — remembering that the tolerance is in the units of the geometry, metres for this dataset. Deleting the islands would discard real data.

2. Your Moran's I test gave a statistic of about 0.59 with an expectation of $-0.018$ and a very small p-value. How do you report it?

Moran's I is not centred on zero; its null expectation is $-1/(n-1)$, here about $-0.018$. A statistic well above that with a small p-value indicates positive autocorrelation. This is the finding that motivates the CAR models in week 10 — if neighbouring counties are similar, a model can borrow strength between them.

3. You built both a binary and a row-standardised weights matrix. Why might the choice change your results?

A county with eight neighbours contributes eight units of weight under a binary scheme and one unit under row standardisation. That changes Moran's I, and it changes what a CAR model smooths towards. Because every result on area-level data depends on the weights matrix, the choice belongs in the methods section rather than being left implicit.
