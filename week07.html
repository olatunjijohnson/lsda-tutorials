Spatial data analysis: week 7 tutorial

Week 7 — Geostatistics and variograms

NoteHow to use this page

Each exercise below is a live R session. Type your answer and press Run; you can experiment freely, nothing is saved and nothing can break. The first block takes a few seconds to start while R loads in your browser.

Worked solutions are on Canvas.

In this tutorial sheet, you will learn the following:

  • how to explore and visualise geostatistical data;
  • how to explore the association between outcomes and covariates;
  • how to construct a variogram; and
  • understand how the spatial correlation can (or cannot be reflected) in the empirical variogram

We analyse the Galicia dataset, often used in geostatistical analysis, refers to lead concentration data from a network of meteorological stations across the Galicia region in Northwest Spain. The Galicia dataset has the following columns:

Column Description
1 Lead concentration
2 Logarithm of lead concentration
3 Longitude
4 Latitude
5 pm10
  1. Load the galicia dataset from the course package and check the columns of the data and ensure that you understand what each column means.
`data(galicia)`. Five columns: the lead concentration and its log, the
coordinates, and PM10. Check which is which before you go further — you will be
modelling the log, not the raw concentration.
What this shows

Five columns: the lead concentration, its logarithm, the two coordinates and PM10. You will model loglead rather than lead, because concentrations are positive and right-skewed, and the Gaussian process the model assumes is symmetric and unbounded.

  1. Map the lead concentration and comment on the design.
Turn the data frame into an `sf` object with `st_as_sf()`, telling it which
columns hold the coordinates and what CRS they are in (these are longitude and
latitude, so EPSG:4326).
Then map it. When you look at the map, ask where the stations are — is the
design regular, clustered, or opportunistic? That governs what you can estimate.
What this shows

The map shows where the 132 stations are, and this is worth more than a moment’s attention. The design determines what the data can support: sparse regions will have wide prediction intervals in week 8, and the near-absence of closely spaced pairs is exactly why the nugget turns out to be unidentifiable there.

Design is not a detail you inherit — if you were planning this survey, this map is what you would be choosing.

  1. Plot the scatter plot of the log of the lead concentration and PM10 . Is there any substantial association between the variables?
An ordinary scatter plot of `loglead` against `pm10`, with a fitted line.
Both are products of traffic and industry, so a positive association would not
be surprising.
What this shows

A positive association, which is unsurprising: lead deposition and PM10 are both products of traffic and industry, so they share their sources.

This matters for the next item. Because PM10 is itself spatially organised, some of what looks like spatial correlation in lead may simply be the effect of PM10. The variogram has to be computed after removing it.

  1. Construct a variogram for residual \(\hat{Z}\) from \[Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 d(x_i) + Z_i,\] where \(Y_i\) is the log-lead concentration and \(d(x)\) is the PM\(_{10}\). Is there evidence of spatial correlation?
Fit `lm(loglead ~ pm10)` first, then build the variogram of its **residuals** —
not of the raw response. The variogram of the response would confound spatial
structure with the trend the covariate explains.
Add a Monte Carlo envelope so you can judge whether the shape is more than noise.
What this shows

The variogram is built from the residuals of the model with the covariate, not from the raw response. That is deliberate: the variogram assumes a constant mean, and any trend left in the data shows up as increasing dissimilarity with distance and imitates spatial correlation.

Read the shape. It rises from near the origin and flattens out, which is the signature of spatial correlation: nearby residuals are alike, distant ones are not. Where it flattens is roughly the range; the height at which it flattens is the sill.

The observed curve falls outside the shaded envelope at short distances. Since the envelope is what you would see if location carried no information, that is evidence of genuine residual spatial structure — and the reason to fit a geostatistical model in week 8 rather than stopping at lm.

  1. Remove the covariate and observe how the variogram changes. Is there any difference in the variogram and what does this imply?
Repeat with `lm(loglead ~ 1)`, that is with no covariate.
Compare the two variograms. If removing the covariate makes the variogram rise
more steeply, the covariate was explaining part of what otherwise looks like
spatial correlation.
What this shows

The same variogram with the covariate removed. It rises more steeply and further.

That difference is the point. PM10 was explaining part of the variation that now appears as spatial correlation. Fit a model without it and the spatial process has to absorb that structure instead — which is precisely what happens to the estimated covariance parameters in week 8.

  1. Perform a confirmatory test for the existence of spatial correlation using the Monte Carlo strategy. Can you confirm an existence of spatial correlation? Is there any effect of the number of permutation on your conclusion?
The envelope test permutes the residuals across locations many times. Under the
null of no spatial correlation, location is irrelevant, so a permuted variogram
is as good as the real one.
The observed curve lying outside the envelope at short distances is evidence of
spatial correlation. Try changing the number of permutations and see how stable
your conclusion is.
What this shows

Changing the number of permutations refines how finely the p-value is resolved — with 99 the smallest attainable is 0.01 — and reduces Monte Carlo noise, but it does not change the underlying evidence.

If your conclusion flips between 99 and 999 permutations, that is informative in itself: the result is marginal and should be reported that way rather than presented as a finding.

Week 7 complete

What you have learnt

You have made the jump from time to space, and met the tool that detects spatial correlation.

  • Geostatistical data. Measurements at a finite set of locations, of a quantity that exists everywhere. That is what makes prediction at unobserved locations a sensible thing to ask for.
  • Design matters. Looking at the map of stations tells you what the data can support. Few close pairs means little information at short range — which will come back in week 8 when the nugget turns out to be unidentifiable.
  • The variogram. Half the mean squared difference between pairs, as a function of the distance between them. Rising then flattening is the signature of spatial correlation.
  • Always on residuals. You built the variogram from the residuals of a model with the covariate, not from the raw response. A trend left in the data looks like spatial correlation and will inflate the variogram.
  • Removing the covariate changed the variogram, which tells you PM10 was explaining part of what would otherwise have been attributed to space.
  • The Monte Carlo envelope test. Permuting values across locations destroys any spatial structure while keeping the marginal distribution. If the observed variogram sits outside the envelope, location matters.

The link back to the first half of the unit: AR(1) in time and the exponential covariance in space are the same idea with a different index.

Check your understanding

Three questions on what you have just done. Not recorded, and you can retry as often as you like.

1. Your variogram of the residuals from `lm(loglead ~ pm10)` rose and then flattened, and the observed curve fell outside the Monte Carlo envelope at short distances. What do you conclude?

The permutation envelope is built by shuffling values across locations, which destroys any spatial structure while leaving the marginal distribution alone. The observed curve sitting outside it means location carries information the covariate has not captured — which is the case for fitting a geostatistical model in week 8 rather than stopping at `lm`.

2. Removing PM10 and recomputing the variogram made it rise more steeply. What does that tell you?

A trend left in the data shows up as increasing dissimilarity with distance and mimics spatial correlation. Because PM10 is itself spatially smooth, dropping it pushes that structure into the residuals. This is why the variogram should always be computed on residuals from the mean model you actually intend to use.

3. You changed the number of permutations from 99 to 999 and the conclusion did not change. What does that indicate?

More permutations give a finer-grained p-value — with 99 the smallest achievable is 0.01 — and reduce Monte Carlo noise, but they do not change the underlying evidence. If a conclusion flips between 99 and 999, that is itself informative: it means the result is marginal and should be reported as such.
