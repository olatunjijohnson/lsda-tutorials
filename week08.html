Week 8 tutorial: Geostatistical modelling and prediction

Week 8 — Kriging and spatial prediction

NoteHow to use this page

Each exercise below is a live R session. Type your answer and press Run; you can experiment freely, nothing is saved and nothing can break. The first block takes a few seconds to start while R loads in your browser.

Worked solutions are on Canvas.

Overview

Last week you established that the Galicia residuals are spatially correlated. This week you put that correlation into a model and use it to predict.

By the end of this sheet you should be able to:

  • fit a linear geostatistical model, with and without a nugget, and say what the difference buys you;
  • interpret the estimated covariance parameters;
  • predict at unobserved locations, with and without a covariate; and
  • compute and map summaries of the predictive distribution, including exceedance probabilities.

The data

The same galicia data as last week: lead concentration measured in moss at 132 sampling stations across Galicia, in north-west Spain, with a co-located measurement of airborne particulate matter.

Column Description
lead lead concentration
loglead log lead concentration — the response
long longitude, decimal degrees
lat latitude, decimal degrees
pm10 particulate matter under 10 micrometres

Two further objects are used for prediction: galicia_grid, a 1 km grid covering the region, and galicia_pred, which holds PM10 at each grid point so that a model with the covariate can be used to predict.

As last week, name the package when you load the data, so load this one with data(galicia, package = "lsda").

Q1: fitting a linear geostatistical model

  1. Fit three linear geostatistical models to the log-transformed lead concentration. In each, \(S(x_i)\) is a stationary Gaussian process with a Matérn correlation function and \(\kappa = 1.5\).

    • Model 1 — no covariate and no nugget: \[\begin{equation}\label{eq:glgm_no_cov} \log(Y_i) = \beta_0 + S(x_i), \end{equation}\]
    • Model 2 — no covariate, but with a nugget: \[\begin{equation}\label{eq:glgm_no_cov_nug} \log(Y_i) = \beta_0 + S(x_i) + Z_i, \end{equation}\] where the \(Z_i\) are independent Gaussian variables with variance \(\tau^2\). What does the estimated nugget tell you about how much it matters here?
    • Model 3 — PM10 as a covariate, no nugget: \[\begin{equation}\label{eq:glgm_lin} \log(Y_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 d(x_i) + S(x_i), \end{equation}\] where \(d(x_i)\) is PM10 at location \(x_i\). How does adding \(d(x_i)\) change the estimated covariance parameters?
Three calls to `sp_fit()`. The first holds the nugget at zero
(`nugget_variance = 0, estimate_nugget = FALSE`), the second lets it be
estimated, and the third adds `pm10` to the formula.
Watch what happens to the nugget estimate and to its confidence interval, and
to the correlation scale when the nugget is forbidden.
What this shows

Three fits, differing only in the formula.

Model 2 versus model 1. Forbidding the nugget does not give a tidier model; it gives a broken one. The log-likelihood falls from 57.82 to 34.08, and the correlation scale collapses from 9.02 km to about a metre. That collapse is the model doing the only thing left to it: with no nugget to hold the measurement-scale variation, the spatial process itself has to become white noise. A likelihood ratio test gives \(2 \times (57.82 - 34.08) = 47.5\) on 1 degree of freedom. The nugget is not optional here.

That does not mean it is well determined. In model 2 the nugget is estimated at 0.0029 with a 95% interval from 0.00004 to 0.23 — four orders of magnitude. The nugget describes behaviour at distances shorter than the closest pair of stations, and the Galicia network has few such pairs, so the likelihood is flat in \(\tau^2\) over a wide range. It is needed, and it is barely identified. If you want to estimate one properly, put close pairs into the design deliberately.

Model 3. PM10 enters with a coefficient around 0.011 (SE 0.0057, \(z = 1.98\)). Note also what happened to the covariance parameters: the process variance falls from 0.0323 to 0.0304 and the correlation scale from 9.02 to 7.86 km. The covariate has absorbed part of the spatial structure, because PM10 is itself spatially smooth. Fixed and random parts of a spatial model are not separable.

One caution before you conclude PM10 matters. The likelihood ratio test gives \(2 \times (59.50 - 57.82) = 3.35\) on 1 degree of freedom, so \(p \approx 0.067\) — much weaker than the Wald test suggests. The Wald test holds the covariance parameters fixed at their estimates while the likelihood ratio test lets them re-estimate, and here they move noticeably. When the two disagree, trust the likelihood ratio test.

  1. Which of the three models do you consider the best? Justify your choice.

  2. Interpret the estimated parameters of the model you chose. Say what each one means in the units of the problem, not just whether it is significant.

`coef()` gives the estimated parameters. The two to interpret are the spatial
process variance and the correlation scale phi.
Phi is not itself the distance at which correlation dies away — convert it to a
practical range, the distance at which correlation falls to about 0.05. For a
Matern with kappa = 1.5 that is roughly 4.7 times phi; solve it properly with
`uniroot()`.
What this shows

\(\phi\) is a scale parameter, not a distance at which correlation dies away — the multiplier depends on the smoothness \(\kappa\). Converting it gives a practical range of about 43 km for model 1 and 37 km for model 3: the distance beyond which correlation has fallen to roughly 0.05.

Report the practical range, not \(\phi\). It is the number a collaborator can interpret, and it is what tells you how far the data can reach when predicting.

  1. Using Model 1 and Model 3, predict log lead concentration across Galicia on a 1 km grid, using galicia_grid for the locations and galicia_pred for PM10 at those locations. Map both the predicted values and their standard errors.
Prediction needs the grid with the covariate at each point (`galicia_pred`).
`sp_predict_draws()` samples from the predictive distribution at every grid
point; `sp_predict_target()` turns those draws into the summaries you want, in
`$summary`.
Map both the predicted mean and the standard error. The second map is the one
that tells you where to trust the first.
What this shows

Two maps, and the second is as important as the first.

The prediction surface is smooth and looks equally confident everywhere, which it is not.

The standard error surface shows why. Uncertainty is low around the stations and rises in the gaps and towards the edges, where prediction becomes extrapolation. The kriging variance is \(\sigma^2 - \mathbf{c}_0^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{c}_0\): near data the subtracted term is large, and far away the correlations decay to nothing and the variance returns to the prior variance, meaning the model has nothing to add beyond the overall mean.

The scale over which that happens is the practical range from item 3 — about 43 km. A location further than that from any station is effectively uninformed.

Presenting the prediction map without the uncertainty map invites exactly the over-confident reading the model was fitted to avoid. If the question is whether lead exceeds a threshold, give an exceedance probability, which uses both.

  1. Compare the two sets of predictions. Where do they agree, where do they differ, and which is more certain?

  2. Where is the predictive uncertainty lowest? Explain why, in terms of the estimated spatial structure and where the sampling stations are.

Challenge

Build a binomial geostatistical model for onchocerciasis (river blindness) prevalence in Liberia, and investigate its relationship with log elevation.

The response is now a count out of a total rather than a continuous measurement, so this extends everything above to the generalised case.

Load the data with data(liberia_oncho, package = "lsda"). The course package also carries an unrelated liberia (malaria and child growth), so the names are deliberately distinct. It holds nodule counts from 90 sampled villages:

Column Description
lat, long latitude and longitude of the village
ntest number of people tested for the presence of nodules
npos number testing positive
elevation village elevation, metres
log_elevation log of elevation
What this shows

sp_fit() fits the binomial model with the Laplace approximation, which is deterministic: refit it as often as you like and the answer does not move. That is worth knowing, because the Monte Carlo maximum likelihood used by some other packages can wander between runs by more than its own reported standard error.

The fit gives 0.337 (SE 0.068) for log elevation, a spatial variance of 0.087 and a range of about 46 km. Doubling elevation multiplies the odds of nodules by \(e^{0.337 \ln 2} = 1.26\) (95% CI 1.15 to 1.39). The standard error is larger than the binomial GLM’s (0.040) because the model now allows for spatial correlation.

Q2: the lecture example, and the question a map is usually asked

The Galicia analysis above predicts a surface. A surface is rarely the decision; the decision is usually whether somewhere is over a limit. This section repeats the modelling on the data from the lectures and then produces the summary that answers that question directly.

gm_no2 holds nitrogen dioxide at 25 monitoring stations across Greater Manchester, with distance to the nearest major road as a covariate, and gm_grid is a 4996-point grid covering the conurbation carrying the same covariate.

  1. Fit the model from the lectures — NO\(_2\) on road distance with a Matérn spatial process — and predict over gm_grid. Map the predictive mean and the predictive standard error.
The covariate is `road`, the distance to the nearest major road in metres.
`sp_predict_draws()` takes the grid itself as `newdata`: it carries both the
locations and the covariates. Drawing over 4996 cells takes a little while --
let it run. Keep the result, because the next question reuses it.
What this shows

The road coefficient is about \(-0.0079\) per metre. Multiply by 1000: every kilometre further from a major road takes about 7.9 units off the predicted NO\(_2\). The spatial process variance is about 105 with a correlation scale of 14.7 km, so there is substantial structure left over and above the road effect.

The predictive mean runs from about 18 to 72 across the grid, and the standard error from 0.26 to 5.3. Compare the two maps: the standard error is smallest near the 25 stations and grows with distance from them, which is the same behaviour you saw for Galicia. A surface that looks equally smooth everywhere is not equally trustworthy everywhere.

Twenty-five stations is a small design for a region this size. That is realistic — monitoring networks are expensive — and it is why the uncertainty map matters more here, not less.

  1. The UK annual mean limit value for NO\(_2\) is 40 µg/m\(^3\). Recompute the prediction summaries to include the probability that each grid cell exceeds it, and map that. How many cells is the model confident about, and how many is it genuinely unsure about?
`sp_predict_draws()` has already drawn samples from the predictive distribution
at every grid cell; `sp_predict_target()` just says how to summarise them, and
`threshold = 40` adds the proportion of draws above 40 — which is the estimated
probability of exceeding it.
Nothing needs refitting: the same `no2.pred` object is being summarised a second
way.
What this shows

Of the 4996 cells, about 4171 have \(P(\text{NO}_2 > 40) > 0.9\), about 404 have it below 0.1, and roughly 421 sit in between.

That last number is the useful one. Those 421 cells are where the data cannot say which side of the limit the truth falls, and they are a different set from “the cells near 40 on the mean map”. A cell whose predictive mean is 45 with a standard error of 5 is genuinely uncertain; a cell whose mean is 45 with a standard error of 0.3 is not. Thresholding the mean map at 40 throws that distinction away, and it is the distinction a regulator needs — it tells you where another monitor would change a decision.

Note what the exceedance map is not: it is not a map of where NO\(_2\) is high. It combines level and uncertainty. A cell can have a low predicted level and a middling exceedance probability if the prediction there is poor, and that is the intended behaviour.

One caution. By default these probabilities are computed with the covariance parameters held at their estimates. They are plug-in probabilities, so they are slightly over-confident, for the same reason plug-in prediction intervals are too narrow. sp_predict_draws() will carry that uncertainty through if you ask it to, with parameter_uncertainty = TRUE; on these data it widens the predictive standard deviations by roughly a tenth and moves a few more cells into the uncertain band. It costs nothing but time, and it is the honest default to reach for when the answer feeds a decision.

  1. Not every dataset has spatial structure worth modelling. Fit a Gaussian geostatistical model to the liberia data in the course package — height-for-age z-scores for 267 clusters, with the local malaria incidence rate as a covariate — and decide from the output whether the spatial term is earning its place.
The covariate is `malaria_incidence_rate`.
Then compare two numbers in the output: the spatial process variance and the
nugget variance. Which of the two is doing the work?
`sp_utm_crs()` picks a sensible projected CRS, because a Mat&eacute;rn
correlation needs distances, and degrees are not distances.
What this shows

Malaria incidence enters at about \(-1.18\) with a standard error of 0.15, so \(p < 10^{-15}\): clusters with higher malaria incidence have markedly lower height-for-age. One unit of incidence costs more than a whole z-score.

Now the covariance parameters, which are the point of the exercise. The spatial process variance is about 0.015 against a measurement error variance of about 0.400 — the spatial term accounts for under 4% of the residual variation. The confidence interval for the spatial variance runs from 0.004 to 0.062, close to the boundary.

So the honest conclusion is that once malaria incidence is in the model there is very little spatial structure left. Compare Galicia, where the spatial variance was the dominant term and dropping it would have been wrong.

This is worth meeting deliberately, because a geostatistical model will always fit. Whether the spatial part is doing anything is a question you have to ask of the output, and the answer is sometimes no. When it is no, say so: a simple regression with independent errors is easier to explain and easier to defend, and the covariate has done the work.

Week 8 complete

What you have learnt

You have fitted geostatistical models and used them to predict.

  • The linear geostatistical model. \(\log(Y_i) = \beta_0 + S(x_i) + Z_i\), with \(S\) a stationary Gaussian process and \(Z_i\) a nugget.
  • The nugget is not free. Its estimate was essentially zero with an infinite upper confidence limit, because the design has almost no close pairs. A parameter you cannot identify is a parameter you should not fit — and if you want to estimate a nugget, put close pairs in the design deliberately.
  • Covariates change the covariance. Adding PM10 reduced both the process variance and the correlation scale: the covariate absorbed part of the spatial structure. Fixed and random parts of a spatial model are not independent of each other.
  • Wald and likelihood ratio tests can disagree. They did here for PM10. The Wald test holds the covariance parameters fixed; the likelihood ratio test lets them re-estimate, which is why it is the safer guide.
  • Phi is not the range. Convert it to a practical range — the distance at which correlation has decayed to about 0.05 — before interpreting it.
  • Kriging, and its uncertainty. Prediction variance is smallest near the data and rises towards the process variance as you move away. The map of standard errors is as important as the map of predictions; showing one without the other invites over-confidence.

Check your understanding

Three questions on what you have just done. Not recorded, and you can retry as often as you like.

1. Model 2 estimated the nugget as 0.00087 with a confidence interval of \([0, \infty)\), and its log-likelihood matched model 1 exactly. What is the right reading?

The nugget describes behaviour at very short distances, so it can only be estimated from pairs of points at those distances. With 132 stations spread across Galicia there are almost none, the likelihood is flat in \(\tau^2\), and the interval is uninformative. The practical lesson is about design: if you need to estimate a nugget, include close pairs deliberately.

2. Adding PM10 reduced the process variance from 0.0323 to 0.0304 and the correlation scale from 9.02 to 7.86 km. What does that show?

This is worth stating in a report: covariance parameters are interpretable only relative to the mean model. A covariate that is itself spatially organised takes over part of the smooth variation, leaving a residual process with less variance and a shorter range. A covariate with no spatial pattern would have little such effect.

3. Your map of prediction standard errors showed low values around the stations and high values in the gaps and at the edges. Why?

The prediction variance is \(\sigma^2 - \mathbf{c}_0^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{c}_0\). Near data the subtracted term is large; far away the correlations vanish and the variance returns to the prior variance, meaning the model has nothing to add beyond the overall mean. The distance over which this happens is set by the practical range — about 43 km here.
